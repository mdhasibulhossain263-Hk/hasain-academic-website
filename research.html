Project 01 · Undergraduate thesis April 2021 – June 2022

Utilisation of rice husk ash as a partial replacement of cement for sustainable concrete

Structural Engineering Laboratory, Presidency University, Dhaka  ·  Supervisor: Susmita Majumder, Lecturer, Department of Civil Engineering  ·  Submitted August 2022

My role. This was a six-member group thesis submitted in partial fulfilment of the B.Sc. in Civil Engineering. I worked on the experimental programme (ash preparation, specimen casting, curing and compression testing) and on the analysis of the strength and cost results presented below. All figures on this page were produced by me from the thesis data.

Problem

Reinforced cement concrete dominates construction in Bangladesh, and cement is both its most expensive constituent and the one with the highest production cost. Rice husk, by contrast, is an agricultural by-product produced at a scale of roughly 1.15 million tonnes of ash per year nationally, and is largely treated as waste. The question was whether ash from that residue could replace part of the cement without losing strength, and whether the saving was large enough to matter.

Method

Rice husk ash was obtained from a rice mill in Bogra, burned at 250–300 °C for two hours and ground. Concrete was batched at a 1 : 1.5 : 3 mix ratio with a water–cement ratio of 0.45, and cement was replaced by RHA at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 % by weight. Cylindrical specimens were cast in three layers with 25 tamps per layer, demoulded after 24 hours and water-cured. Three specimens per mix were tested in compression at 7, 14 and 28 days, giving 54 specimens in total.

Principal findings

  • 5 % replacement raised 28-day compressive strength by 21.8 %, from 2,724 psi for the control mix to 3,317 psi. This was the clearest result in the study.
  • Strength fell sharply beyond 10 %: 15 % replacement retained only 56 % of control strength, and 25 % retained 32 %.
  • The ash was strongly siliceous, at 62.3 % SiO2 against 20.5 % for the ordinary Portland cement it replaced, which is the basis of its pozzolanic behaviour.
  • Water absorption rose with replacement level, from 2.63 % at 0 % RHA to 4.32 % at 25 %, and slump fell from 2.4 cm to 2.0 cm: a durability and workability cost that sets the practical ceiling.
  • Material cost fell modestly, by 1.3 % at 5 % replacement and 2.5 % at 10 %, since RHA cost 3.33 tk/kg against 11 tk/kg for cement.
  • Recommended range: 5–10 % replacement, which is where strength is maintained or improved while the absorption penalty stays small.

What the numbers mean at building scale

The thesis extrapolated the 10 % case to a two-storey 50 × 50 ft building requiring roughly 6,500 cft of concrete, giving a material saving of about 66,200 BDT. That is a real number for a small contractor, though it is a linear extrapolation from cylinder costs rather than a measured site figure, and it excludes the cost of collecting, burning and grinding the husk at scale.

What I would do differently

Three things. First, characterise the ash properly, with XRD to confirm the silica is amorphous rather than crystalline, which the burning temperature makes uncertain. Second, test durability directly: chloride ingress and carbonation depth matter more than water absorption in a delta environment. Third, carry out a proper life-cycle assessment of the embodied carbon, which this study did not attempt and which is the question I most want to answer next.

Project 02 · Geotechnical laboratory March 2020 – January 2021

Column behaviour modelling, custom instrumentation and feasibility analysis for granular pile systems

Geotechnical Engineering Laboratory, Presidency University, Dhaka  ·  Supervisor: Prof. Dr. Tushar Ahamed

This was the second phase of a two-year programme on granular pile foundations. The first phase had established the soil parameters and a baseline numerical model; this phase concentrated on the behaviour of the column itself under load, and on whether the system was defensible economically as well as technically.

Why column behaviour is the whole problem

A granular pile carries load in a way that is fundamentally different from a concrete pile. It has no tensile strength and no bond with the surrounding ground. A column of compacted stone can only resist load because the soil around it pushes back. The confining pressure the surrounding soil can mobilise is what stops the column spreading sideways under load. In soft soil, which is precisely where ground improvement is needed, that confinement is weak. The capacity of the pile is therefore governed by the strength of the soil, not of the stone.

That is why the column deforms rather than breaks, and why a granular pile can fail in three quite different ways depending on its length-to-diameter ratio and the stratigraphy it sits in. Designing one means identifying which mechanism governs, which is what the modelling in this phase was for.

What I did

  • Designed custom instrumentation for the test programme, where standard apparatus did not cover the measurements the study required.
  • Conducted geotechnical testing to ASTM standards, generating the experimental dataset against which the numerical models were checked.
  • Developed column behaviour models in PLAXIS 3D, simulating load transfer, deformation and the failure mechanisms shown above.
  • Analysed experimental and numerical data together to assess both technical performance and economic feasibility.
  • Trained and mentored undergraduate students in experimental design, data analysis and numerical modelling. This was my first sustained teaching responsibility in a research setting.

On designing the instrumentation

Standard geotechnical apparatus is built for standard tests. A model granular pile under load is not one of those: you need to know how the column deforms along its length, not simply what load it failed at, and no catalogue cell gives you that.

Building the rig myself forced me to specify the measurement from first principles: what has to be resolved, to what precision, how the apparatus itself deforms under the load it is measuring, and where that error ends up in the result. It is the single most useful habit I took out of the laboratory, and the one I have used most in field assessment work since.

Project 03 · Geotechnical laboratory February 2019 – January 2020

Finite element simulation of reclaimed asphalt pavement – infused granular piles

Geotechnical Engineering Laboratory, Presidency University, Dhaka  ·  Supervisor: Prof. Dr. Tushar Ahamed

Reclaimed asphalt pavement is milled from roads during resurfacing and is generated in large quantities wherever a road network is maintained. It is normally treated as waste or downgraded to low-value fill. The question in this project was whether RAP could serve as the granular fill in a load-bearing pile used to improve soft ground, a use that would put a waste stream to structural work and reduce demand for quarried aggregate at the same time.

Why RAP is a plausible pile fill

As Figure 6 shows, a granular pile stands or falls on the shear strength of its fill and on the confinement the surrounding soil can provide. The fill therefore has to be well graded, angular, frictional and durable. RAP already satisfies most of that specification, because it was engineered once to those requirements for a road pavement and is recovered largely intact.

The complication is the residual bitumen coating the particles. It can act as a lubricant at the grain contacts, reducing the friction angle on which the column's capacity depends, and it makes the material time- and temperature-dependent in a way clean stone is not. Whether the gain in grading and angularity outweighs that loss in friction is an empirical question, which is why the programme began with laboratory characterisation rather than with modelling.

What I did

  • Determined the soil parameters through ASTM-compliant laboratory testing (grading, Atterberg limits, specific gravity, compaction and shear strength), using instrumentation I designed for the purpose. These measured values, not literature defaults, became the model input.
  • Built PLAXIS 3D finite element models simulating the behaviour of RAP-infused granular piles under load, so that geometries and load cases could be explored beyond what the physical test programme could cover.
  • Conducted comprehensive literature reviews on granular pile design and on reclaimed asphalt as a geotechnical material, and contributed to the thesis proposal that framed the second phase of the programme.

The pairing matters: the laboratory work fixes the parameters and the model extends them. Neither is worth much alone, and the point of the exercise was learning where a finite element result stops being trustworthy, which is exactly at the quality of the soil parameters fed into it.

The circular-economy argument

A granular pile needs a well-graded, frictional, durable fill. RAP has those properties already, having been engineered once for a road and recovered intact. Using it as pile fill avoids both the quarrying of new aggregate and the disposal of the milled material. This is the same reasoning I later applied professionally in material traceability and circular economy research for GIZ: identifying a waste stream whose existing engineering properties make it valuable rather than merely disposable.

Project 04 · Environmental laboratory June 2018 – December 2018

Artificial neural network forecasting of industrial impacts on urban river water quality

Environmental Engineering Laboratory, Presidency University, Dhaka  ·  Supervisor: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Asma Akter

Dhaka's rivers receive discharge from dense industrial clusters, and the relationship between that discharge and downstream water quality is neither linear nor well captured by simple regression. This was my first research project, and it set the direction for everything that followed: the question was whether a data-driven model could forecast water quality from industrial loading well enough to be useful for environmental management.

The hardest part was not the modelling. It was assembling the dataset: twenty years of surface water quality records held across different government agencies, in different formats, with different sampling regimes. Compiling and reconciling that archive took most of the project, and taught me that in environmental research the data acquisition is the research.

Why a neural network, rather than a regression

River water quality downstream of an industrial cluster is not a linear function of discharge. The governing parameters interact: temperature affects dissolved oxygen, which affects how organic load is processed; flow dilutes concentration but also mobilises sediment; monsoon seasonality overlays all of it. A linear model forces you to specify those interactions in advance, and in a system this coupled you do not know them in advance.

A feed-forward network makes no such commitment. It learns the mapping from inputs to output from the records themselves, which is the right tool when the mechanism is known to be non-linear but its form is not. The cost is interpretability, since the model predicts without explaining, which is why the correlation and time-series analysis came first: that stage identified which parameters mattered, and the network then handled how they combined.

20 yr Surface-water quality records compiled across government agencies
ANN Feed-forward architecture for non-linear forecasting

What this project set in motion

It established the method I have used since: quantify the environmental effect first, then argue about the intervention. It is also the direct reason I am applying to Environmental Science rather than continuing purely in structural engineering: the problem that interested me was not the structure, it was what the structure was doing to the river.

What I would do differently

Hold out a validation period properly, rather than judging fit on the data the model was trained on. Test the network against a simpler baseline, because a multiple regression that performs nearly as well is the better answer. And treat the gaps in the archive explicitly, since twenty years of agency records contain missing seasons, and how those are handled changes the result more than the choice of model does.
